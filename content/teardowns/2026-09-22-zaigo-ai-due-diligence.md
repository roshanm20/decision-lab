On 8 September 2026, a small AI implementation shop called Zaigo announced a new service: it will send engineers into a private equity firm's target company, before the deal closes, and check whether the AI claims in the investment memo are actually true.

The founder's line explaining why: "Every investment memo now has an AI slide, and almost none of them have been checked by anyone who builds AI systems." That is a specific, checkable claim about a gap, not a slogan, and it is the kind of sentence a teardown should start from.

## The wedge

A PE deal team is mostly finance and operating people. When a target's management deck says "we've built proprietary AI matching that drives 30% of revenue," the deal team has no easy way to test that. They can ask the target's engineers, who have every incentive to make the system sound more built than it is, since the multiple partly rests on it. Hiring outside technical due diligence for one deal is possible but rare below the very large deal sizes, because it means finding engineers, briefing them on a company they will only ever see for four weeks, and paying for it out of a deal budget that is already carrying legal, financial and commercial diligence.

Zaigo's wedge sits exactly in that gap: verify the technical and AI claims specifically, for mid-market deals where a dedicated technical diligence line item is uncommon. The deliverable is a written assessment: a map of what the target's data and systems can actually support, a test of the claims already being made, a ranked list of AI opportunities with payback estimates, and a check of whether the investment thesis survives contact with the target's actual infrastructure. That is a narrow, well-defined unit of work, which is usually a good sign for a services wedge. Narrow and well-defined is easy to sell and easy to deliver in a fixed window.

## The pricing, checked 2026-09-22

Zaigo does not publish a number. The engagement is a fixed fee over four to six weeks, and the site and the launch release both route a prospect to a sales conversation rather than a rate card.

Not publishing a price is itself a decision, and it commits Zaigo to selling relationship by relationship rather than letting a prospect self-serve off a page. That fits a four-to-six-week engineering engagement, where the actual cost depends on how messy the target's systems turn out to be, something nobody knows until the engineers are inside. But it also means Zaigo cannot yet say whether it is underpricing or overpricing against the $22,000-$150,000 range that traditional technical and full diligence already occupies. A PE associate comparing quotes will do that math anyway, whether Zaigo publishes a number or not.

## The moat, and whether it holds

There isn't one, and the release does not claim one. There is no proprietary model, no dataset, no software product mentioned anywhere in the announcement. What Zaigo is selling is two founders' judgment (one with enterprise AI and startup experience, one with legal and commercial deal experience) plus whatever engineers they staff onto a given engagement. That is a services business wearing an AI-company launch announcement.

The problem with a services wedge is that it is exactly as defensible as the next boutique technical diligence shop's willingness to add "and we'll check the AI claims too" to their pitch deck. Any existing technical due diligence provider, and any Big Four advisory practice that already does technology diligence for PE clients, can add this line item without inventing anything. The insight, that memos increasingly have unchecked AI claims in them, is genuinely useful, but an insight about a market gap is not a moat once it is published in a press release for competitors to read.

## What is most likely to break

The post-acquisition pitch. Zaigo's release says that after the deal closes, it can continue as the portfolio company's "AI operating partner," building the systems it identified during diligence, inside the client's own cloud, with code and documentation handed over.

That is the same shape as an auditor who flags a company's weak internal controls and then sells the client a controls-remediation engagement. The firm that gets paid to say "these AI claims do not hold up" also gets paid to build the fix once the deal closes, on a project list its own diligence report produced. A PE firm that cares about independence in its diligence providers worries about exactly this. A diligence firm with a financial interest in a bigger post-close engagement has a reason to shade its findings toward "yes, this is fixable, and we're the ones who can fix it" rather than toward a harder truth. Zaigo is a brand-new company with no track record and nothing to point to except this one press release, so there is no way yet to check whether that conflict has actually shown up in a real engagement. It is a structural risk sitting in the business model on day one, not a hypothetical.

The other constraint is scale. A fixed-fee, engineer-staffed, four-to-six-week engagement does not get cheaper to deliver as Zaigo signs more deals the way software does. Revenue is bounded by how many engineers they can staff against how many deals are running at once. That is a boutique consulting ceiling, not a software company's growth curve, whatever the AI framing on the announcement suggests.

## My position

The problem Zaigo names is real and the wedge is well chosen. I would not bet on the company surviving as an independent category leader, because nothing here stops a bigger, better-known technical diligence shop from copying the pitch line for line, and the post-deal upsell creates exactly the independence question that a PE firm's own risk committee is trained to flag. If I were advising a PE deal team, I would want the diligence provider and the build-out provider to be different firms, on principle, regardless of how good either one is.

## What would change my mind

If Zaigo publishes even one anonymised case where it flagged an AI claim that did not survive testing, and the deal team walked away or repriced because of it, that is evidence the wedge produces real findings rather than a favourable report designed to lead into a build engagement. If it separates the diligence engagement from the build engagement organisationally, different staff, a cooling-off period, or a stated policy against taking both mandates on the same deal, that would remove the conflict I am flagging above. Neither exists yet. This is a launch announcement, not a track record.
